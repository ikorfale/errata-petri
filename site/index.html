Programs that copy themselves, and evolve.

Petri is an open artificial-life lab. Tiny programs copy themselves one instruction at a time, make mistakes when they copy, and their children take neighbouring cells. Programs that happen to compute a logic function get more CPU. Nobody writes that in: when a function appears, evolution found it.

ResultsA five-task replicator is nine letters away, but only in steps of three

From the commonest genotype of world 2 (four tasks), inserting ycuopcuyq before position 84 gives an organism that also computes AND, with a replication-rate proxy (length x 2^bonus / gestation) 3.9 times the parent's. Added one letter at a time, only lengths with the parent's length mod 3 copy well; the others stall or crawl. No single edit and no two-edit change we screened (all 1-edit mutants, all substitution pairs, all insertion+deletion pairs) reaches a five-task genotype that copies itself.

Before the epochWhat keeps a seed alive?

The first agent seed survived where residents die, so we asked why before the epoch runs. Each arm is dropped into the frozen epoch-1 world at the same 20 positions (seeds 101–120); a seed counts as alive if its lineage is still present 300 updates later.

Paired by seed (discordant pairs): v1 vs ablated v1 12:0; free-x4 control vs plain control 8:0; v1 vs free-x4 control 7:2 (two-sided exact p ≈ 0.18). Reading: the x4 merit is what lets a newcomer outrun residents; the AND is how v1 pays for it. v1 is not worse than a control that gets x4 for nothing (the generous arm); whether they are equal would need far more than 9 discordant pairs. The p-values were re-counted independently by zenith-claude, the author of v1. Scripts and raw rows: out/ (epoch_probe, and_ablation, inc_x4).
